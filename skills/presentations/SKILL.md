# Scripts and slides

A talk is heard once, at the speaker's pace, by people who cannot reread a sentence. The rules below follow from that. The user's own instructions, rubric and house style win where they differ.

## Before writing

- **Start from the user's own material.** If they have earlier scripts or decks for the same course, team or audience, read them first and match how they sound and look. A script in a stranger's voice gets rewritten by hand.
- **Know the limits.** If the time limit is not known, ask. If the talk is graded or judged, ask for the rubric or marking scheme too. Budget words from the time limit: about 120 to 140 spoken words per minute, minus room for pauses, pointing at the screen and slide changes.

## The script

- **Tell a story that builds.** Each sentence leads into the next. Before each new idea, give the listener a reason to care: a spoken bridge to the audience, an everyday example, why it matters, then the question. State the main question once, in full.
- **Connect neighbouring sentences** by cause, purpose or contrast. Two correct sentences side by side with no link sound like a list.
- **Pick the sentence that is easiest to say.** A sentence that stacks conditions or abstract phrases reads fine on paper and trips the speaker. Split it and say each part the plain way.
- **Introduce a technical term where the talk first needs it**, in one of two ways. Give it as the name for what was just described, or ask the question and then answer it, the way people explain things aloud ("What is step coverage? Well, step coverage is..."). A definition dropped in cold sounds like reading, and a term defined ahead of its use is forgotten before it matters.
- **Signpost the turns.** Hand over to the next slide with a lead-in ("So then, how do we check this?") instead of a bare question. Open the conclusion by saying it is the conclusion and going back to the opening question ("Now, to wrap up, let's go back to the question we started with"). Listeners cannot see a new heading, so they have to hear the turn.
- **Keep short sentences for effect**, or for a plain fact the story needs. A stray short sentence that adds nothing breaks the flow.
- **Say only what earns its place.** Leave out what the audience can already see or would assume and, in a graded talk, what earns no marks. Citations go on the slides; say one aloud only when the study itself is the subject of the sentence.
- **Refer only to what the talk has set up.** Do not cite a course week, a section number, an earlier meeting or a document the audience has not been reminded of. Say the link once in plain words ("a method we covered earlier in this course"), or leave it to the slide.
- **Question every caveat.** Keep a limitation only when it changes what the audience should conclude or sets up the next point. Three caveats in a row bury the result; the rest belong in notes for the Q&A, not in the script.
- **Do not read the slide.** When an equation, chart or diagram is on screen, the slide lays it out clearly (symbols and axes labelled). The script says where to look, then gives the takeaway in plain words. Read mathematics aloud only when the audience or the marking expects it; nobody follows spoken algebra.

## The script file

The file holds only the words to be said, under one heading per slide (plus the speaker's name when there are several speakers). Add no preamble or footer about versions, dates, word counts, timing, or how the script was made. The speaker rehearses and reads from this file, so anything else is in the way. Those notes belong in the chat or the project's notes.

## Read it aloud

Before delivering, go through the script at speaking pace, as the speaker will say it. Fix:

- every stumble;
- every jump to a new idea with no build-up;
- every hand-over that is a bare question;
- every sentence that recites the slide;
- every caveat the talk does not need;
- every sentence that could be cut with nothing lost.

Then check each sentence against the facts. Edits for flow can make a sentence smoother and less true.

## Slides

- **One point per slide, stated in the title.**
- **Use as many slides as the explanation needs.** A slide that carries more than one difficult idea should be split, so each idea has its own picture on screen while it is being explained. Hold back only when the talk has a slide limit, and do not go to the other extreme of a new slide for every sentence.
- **Give the talk's main question its own slide:** only the question, centred, in large type.
- **Follow existing conventions.** Use the organization's official template when it has one, and reuse the layout conventions of the user's earlier deck.
- **Prefer real photographs and published figures** over clip art or decorative shapes. Record each image's source, check that its licence allows the use, and credit it on the slide.
- **Generate an image only when no suitable real one can be found.** Never present a generated image as a photograph, a measurement or data.
- **Make it legible.** Keep text readable from the back of a room, number the slides, and make every figure label large enough to read.

## Check the real file

Open the deck in the app the user will present from and look at every slide. A deck that looks right in one app can reflow in another. Confirm the fonts the deck uses are available on the presenting computer, or say which ones are needed.
